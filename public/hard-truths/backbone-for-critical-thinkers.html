A Backbone for the Critical Thinkers

Vlad Cealicu // 25 October 2026

You need more than critical thinking to build a society I'd want to live in.

> EPISODE 22 // OFFLINE-READY NOTEBOOKLM AUDIO
00:00 / --:-- DOWNLOAD

When I wrote down what I mean by critical thinking for The Age That Needs Critical Thinkers, I ended up with a long list, and most of it wasn't critical thinking at all. It only made sense to write when I realised that you need more than critical thinking to build a society I'd want to live in. Asking why five times and spotting the leap in an argument are ways to think, and on their own they make you a very good arguer. The rest is closer to ethics, or to a way to live in a world where fewer people go to church, be it lack of time or not believing, and where AI sits next to all of us while we think.

> 1. How I want to treat people

  1. Kindness and charity.
  2. A temporary fix with a long-term plan behind it that deals with the root cause.
  3. Understanding that we succeed together, and that leaders wouldn't be leaders without everyone else working hard to make their vision real.
  4. Treating other people with respect regardless of who they are, and doing to others as you'd want done to you, without necessarily going to church every week, or ever.
  5. Avoiding, as much as possible, doing things that harm or disrupt other people, while giving them the privacy to do whatever they want in their own lives under the same rule. John Stuart Mill wrote it down in 1859, "the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others" [1].
  6. Leaving room for people who argue for things completely against what you believe, and trusting that a society with a strong critical thinking backbone can choose correctly.

> 2. Where I draw the lines

  1. Strong moral values, and an open mind that still has lines it won't cross.
  2. Accepting that we need regulation to protect us from predatory practices while we build up our critical thinking.
  3. On paper the best system of governance is a benevolent dictatorship, because it allows a quick response to a world that changes fast, but that's only because the general population was never expected to develop critical thinking and was expected to follow the rules. In real life it breaks when power accumulates, which it always tends to, sometimes within a few years and sometimes over a generation. A dictator ends up building their own dictator brain out of people who only tell them they're right (the AI version of the same loop is in How to Prevent Building Your Own Dictator Brain). It's funny, because I was writing the notes for this in September in Preveza, which happens to be where Rome's emperors started. I went to see the ruins of Nicopolis, the city Octavian founded to celebrate his win at Actium, a few years before he became Augustus, the first of them [2]. Rome's five good emperors later handed power on by adoption until the fifth, Marcus Aurelius, left it to his son Commodus, whose reign is usually taken as the end of the Pax Romana [3]. It happens to elected leaders too. David Owen and Jonathan Davidson went through a hundred years of US presidents and UK prime ministers and suggested a name for what they found, hubris syndrome, "a disorder of the possession of power", particularly power "held for a period of years and with minimal constraint on the leader" [4].
  4. Choosing democracy anyway because, even with low critical thinking, it's still better in the long term. Churchill made the case for it in the House of Commons in 1947, "democracy is the worst form of Government except for all those other forms that have been tried from time to time", and presented it as something that had been said before [5].
  5. Human rights exist because we want them to exist. We believe in them because we choose to, and the choosing is what makes them real. Yuval Noah Harari puts it as "There are no gods in the universe, no nations, no money, no human rights, no laws, and no justice outside the common imagination of human beings" [6].

> 3. How to live with it

  1. Knowing that the world is broken, that it always has been and that your impact will be small, and still doing something.
  2. Fighting when you know you're the underdog.
  3. Trying, even though "you can be anything you put your mind to" is bullshit.
  4. Working hard, knowing it's a big part of success but not the only part.
  5. Knowing that sometimes your sacrifice is the best way forward, and knowing when to take a step back.
  6. Finding meaning and joy in just existing, journey before destination, as the Knights Radiant swear in Brandon Sanderson's The Way of Kings [7].

> 4. Is this still critical thinking?

Only some of this is critical thinking, and a lot of it is borrowed, from Mill, Churchill, Harari and the golden rule. I'm putting it together because I think it works better as one thing than as separate pieces.

I might be wrong about some of it, and part of critical thinking is being open to being persuaded, so if you think I am, reach out at me@vladcealicu.com. If someone has already written all of this down in one place, I'd like to read it. [ localghost.ai // hard-truths ]
