The Class of 2026

Vlad Cealicu // 22 November 2026

On Friday 20 November 2026 I was in York doing a walking tour of the city centre, and when we got to York Minster the new graduates from York St John University were having their graduation ceremony [1]. It made me think of mine, of the sense of accomplishment and the sense of dread about what to do next.

I think they are graduating into a much bleaker job market than I did, and I graduated in 2010, just after the financial crisis, and got my job in London in 2011. The stats don't say that, because unemployment was higher then [2] and the big employers hired a few more graduates this year than last [3], but the stats and what I see around me and on the internet don't line up at all. I think the stats and numbers are not catching up as fast as the changes are happening, and that it's much worse than they show.

> The data does not add up

This year's graduates can't be in the stats yet, because the official survey of graduates asks them what they're doing 15 months after they finish, so the newest results are about the class of 2024, who were asked at the end of 2025, and the class of 2026 won't be asked until September 2027 at the earliest [4]. The 2025 stats did not hit as hard, but you'll see it once the stats for 2026 come out. American payroll data already shows it getting worse, with Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen putting people aged 22 to 25 in the jobs most exposed to AI 19% behind people the same age in other jobs in June 2026, up from 15% a year earlier [5].

We are also probably not counting NEETs (young people not in education, employment or training) the same way as we used to. In 2011, the year I got my job, more than half of them were looking for work and counted as unemployed, and now six in ten are not looking, so they are left out of the unemployment rate [6]. The survey that rate comes from had so few people answering it that for two and a half years the ONS labelled its figures as in development and advised caution with them, and it says more people are in work than a year ago while the tax records say fewer are on a payroll [7].

I think payroll is still lagging as well, and some of what it shows will be companies choosing a cheaper person for the same job, since the cheaper person can use AI as well as the other one, and paying them less [8].

It could also be that people are taking more jobs that have nothing to do with their degree, since each of them counts as employed, and more than one in five of the class of 2024 said they weren't using what they learnt in what they do now [4].

The numbers we do have show 141 applications for every graduate position when in 2002 it was 38 [3], graduate job adverts at their lowest for the time of year since 2020 [9] and vacancies across the UK at their lowest since 2014 outside the pandemic [10].

> What happened to software

Over the last year, closer to two, software development has changed more than at any time in my almost 20 years of writing software for money. We're still problem solvers at heart, and I think the passion I kept preaching is still the driving factor, but very few people still write code [11] and the layoffs have intensified [12]. I wrote about the juniors in March 2026, in We Sold a Generation a Dream and Dismantled the Ladder While They Climbed, when London tech had stopped training them. I think what happened to software is a playbook that will be repeated in all the other industries, the juniors first and then a bit less work for everyone else, and The Office Used to Hire Everyone is about that.

> Why I think this is the time to build

That sounds bleak and hopeless, but it's not, because I think this generation, and all the other ones for that matter, has the biggest opportunity to build new things and to test new theories. Your obsession that used to be close to impossible to follow can now be followed, and you have the chance to build whatever you want, including the new social systems for the world after AGI. Between 3D printers, the progress in robotics and LLMs, you have all the tools to be an entrepreneur.

It sounds daunting, and it's much easier said than done, but it's what the world needs now, critical thinkers, entrepreneurs and people who take risks (The Age That Needs Critical Thinkers is about the first of those, and One Bad Quarter about why now is the time to start). In The Office Used to Hire Everyone I said to start exploring while you still have the wage, and if you're graduating now you don't have the wage yet, so if you can take advantage of a safety net, be it your parents, social programmes or whatever else, do it and build something. Build it because you love building it, and because you have to flex the entrepreneur muscle.

> The other option is research

The other option is becoming a researcher, because as we get more theories about the physical world, in physics, biology and chemistry, we'll need people in the field to test them and to prove or disprove them. When Google surveyed 637 scientists in the summer of 2026, more than four in ten said what holds their research back has moved to the lab work, the clinical trials and the data collection, and about the same share said their backlog of untested ideas is growing [13]. That work is what universities should be teaching, and my guess is that it's where more than half of the white collar jobs will go, to research labs.

If you graduated at York Minster that week, congratulations, and if you're building something, or have ideas about any of this, reach out at me@vladcealicu.com. [ localghost.ai // hard-truths ]
